Lesson 7 · 16 min

Autonomy by evidence, not by trust

The temptation is to grant autonomy early: the answers look good and someone says "just let it run on its own." It's like promoting the intern on day two because they asked a good question. This lesson gives you the four levels, the numbers to move up, and the way back.

Start the lesson

In this lesson you will

  1. 1The four levels: recommends, executes with approval, executes with exceptions, operates within limits
  2. 2The numbers to move up a level
  3. 3When to step down, and why it isn't punishment
  4. 4Practice the conversation with someone who wants to skip steps

Goal: decide when to raise or lower an agent's autonomy level using accuracy, run volume, and the severity of errors.

01 · The four levels of autonomy

One task at a time. Click each level to see the criteria for moving up.

In the project's personal life and Jarvis texts, the ladder appears with three steps (proposes, executes and you review, executes and reports). It's the same ladder: here it's complete, with the approval level shown separately.

The criterion for moving up isn't a suggestion: it's a requirement. Without the numbers, the promotion doesn't happen.

02 · What to measure before promoting

Eight metrics. The first three are the minimum.

1. Volume and accuracy

History of runs in that scenario and accuracy rate by evaluation (human or automated).

2. Severity and cost

Not every error is equal: getting the tone of a message wrong is not the same as getting the amount on an invoice wrong. How much does each failure cost in money, time, reputation, or rework?

3. Stability

Does the rate hold over time or swing? Does it keep quality across varied scenarios, not just simple ones? How often does someone need to correct it?

4. The reverse path

If an agent at level 3 starts making mistakes in a new way (the scenario changed, a rule is outdated, an integration broke), it moves down to level 2 until it stabilizes.

Moving down isn't punishment. It's the cycle working.
